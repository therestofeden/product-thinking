# PRISM — Should We Build This?

PRISM is a five-phase framework for evaluating whether a company should build a product. It moves from problem space to real players to a committed insight to a solution to how you measure whether you were right.

Each phase has a specific job. Run them in order. The discipline is in committing — PRISM forces you to converge, not to enumerate.

```
P — Problem Space     Define the market, mission fit, and opportunity size. Why should this product exist? Why for this company?
R — Real Players      Map user segments, in-house capabilities, external competitors. Should we build or buy?
I — Insights          Commit to one segment and one insight. Defend it.
S — Solution          Define the MVP and the 3-year product. Map both to company mission.
M — Measure           MVP metrics that confirm the hypothesis. Long-term North Star. Guardrails.
```

---

## Orientation: Gather Context First

Before running PRISM, understand the setup:

1. **What is the product or space being evaluated?** One sentence.
2. **What company are we evaluating this for?** What is their mission, existing product surface, business model?
3. **What is the context?** (Interview question, leadership pitch, internal strategy review, build vs. buy decision)
4. **What do we already know about the problem space?** Prior research, customer signals, competitor moves?
5. **What constraints apply?** (Regulatory, timeline, team capability, geographic scope)

If this is an interview context: research the company's mission, North Star, existing products, and strategic themes before running PRISM. The strength of PRISM depends entirely on how tightly each phase is grounded in that company's specific context.

## P — Problem Space

*Define the market, the mission fit, and the opportunity size. Answer: why should this product exist, and why for this company specifically?*

### What this phase delivers

Three things, in order:

**1. Market definition.** What is the problem being solved, for whom, at what scale? Avoid the common trap of defining the market too broadly ("the future of healthcare") or too narrowly ("doctors at small clinics who use feature X"). Define it at the level of the real problem: who is underserved, what does underserved look like in practice, and how large is that population?

**2. Mission alignment.** Does this product strengthen the company's mission, or is it adjacent? The test: if this initiative succeeds, does it make the company's North Star move? A product that generates revenue but is orthogonal to the North Star is a distraction. A product that addresses the same fundamental problem from a new angle is an extension. Name which this is, and why.

**3. Opportunity sizing.** How large is the market? Approach sizing from the bottom up: number of addressable customers × annual value per customer. State which assumptions are [KNOWN] (measured), [BELIEVED] (theory + proxy evidence), and [ASSUMED] (required for the logic but unvalidated). A financial model is a belief system until validated — label it accordingly.

### Questions to answer

- What is the problem? For whom? What does their current workaround look like?
- Is this a problem the company's mission equips them to solve — in terms of data, distribution, trust, or capability?
- What is the market size? State the assumptions. Which ones are [ASSUMED]?
- Why now? What has changed that makes this the right moment?
- What is the failure condition? What would "building this and failing" look like — for the company's North Star, not just the product P&L?

> **Format:**
>
> **The problem:** [2–3 sentences. What fails, for whom, through what mechanism.]
>
> **Mission fit:** [Does solving this strengthen or dilute the company's North Star? One sentence with reasoning.]
>
> **Market size:** [Bottom-up estimate with epistemic labels on key assumptions. TAM → SAM → SOM where relevant.]
>
> **Why now:** [What structural shift makes this the right moment — regulatory, technological, behavioral, competitive.]
>
> **Failure condition:** [What looks like success on the wrong metric. Name it before moving forward.]

## R — Real Players

*Map who the users are, what capabilities exist in-house, and who the external players are. Answer: should we build or buy — and if build, can we win?*

### What this phase delivers

Three lenses:

**1. User segments and pain points.** Who are the real users of this product? For each segment: what is the specific pain point, what is the current workaround, and what would they sacrifice to solve it? Do not list all possible users — identify the 2–3 segments that matter most and be specific about what makes their pain real.

**2. In-house capabilities.** What does the company already have that is relevant? This includes: existing user relationships and trust, proprietary data, technical infrastructure, distribution channels, regulatory standing, team capabilities. The question is not "could we build this from scratch?" but "what unfair advantage does this company have that an outsider does not?" If the honest answer is "none," that is important to surface.

**3. External competitive landscape.** Who already plays in this space? Rate each player on: market share, solution maturity, and why a customer would switch. The goal is not an exhaustive competitive audit — it is understanding the switching cost and where the gaps are.

**Build or buy decision.** Based on the above: is the fastest and most defensible path to building in-house, acquiring a player, partnering with one, or licensing? What does each option cost in time, money, and strategic risk?

> **Format:**
>
> **User segments:**
> | Segment | Pain point | Current workaround | Switching motivation |
> |---|---|---|---|
>
> **In-house advantages:** [Bullet list. Be honest — list only genuine advantages, not aspirational ones.]
>
> **External players:**
> | Player | Solution maturity | Moat | Why customers would switch |
> |---|---|---|---|
>
> **Build / Buy / Partner recommendation:** [One of the three, with a one-paragraph rationale covering time-to-market, capability gap, and strategic defensibility.]
>
> **Leadership pitch angle:** [One sentence framing why this initiative makes sense to a CEO/CPO who controls resources. What is the strategic narrative?]

## I — Insights

*Commit to one segment and one insight. Defend it.*

### What this phase delivers

The hardest part of PRISM is this one. Everything up to here has been analysis. Now you commit.

**One segment.** Of the segments identified in R, which one do you prioritise? The answer is not "all of them" and not "start with the easiest one." The answer is the segment where your in-house advantage is strongest, the pain is highest, and the switching motivation is clearest. Name it and defend the choice.

**One insight.** An insight is not an observation. It is a non-obvious truth about that segment's behaviour, need, or constraint that explains why existing solutions fail them — and that points directly at what a good solution would look like. A good insight is specific, defensible, and falsifiable. "Doctors are busy" is not an insight. "Independent GPs lose 40% of bookable appointment slots to no-shows, but no penalty mechanism exists that patients would accept without destroying trust" is an insight.

**The defence.** Having named the segment and the insight, articulate:
- What evidence supports this insight? [KNOWN] / [BELIEVED] / [ASSUMED]
- What would invalidate it? (The riskiest assumption)
- Why hasn't an existing player solved it? (The structural gap)

> **Format:**
>
> **Committed segment:** [Name + one sentence on why this segment, not the others.]
>
> **The insight:** [One sentence. Non-obvious. Falsifiable. Points at what a good solution looks like.]
>
> **Evidence:** [KNOWN / BELIEVED / ASSUMED label + source or reasoning]
>
> **Riskiest assumption:** [What would prove this insight wrong?]
>
> **Why existing solutions miss it:** [The structural reason incumbents don't solve this.]

## S — Solution

*Define the MVP and the 3-year product. Map both to the company's mission.*

### What this phase delivers

**The MVP.** Not the smallest thing you could ship. The smallest thing that tests whether the core insight is correct. The MVP has one job: validate or invalidate the riskiest assumption from the Insights phase. It is not feature-complete. It does not need to be beautiful. It needs to tell you whether you were right about the insight.

For the MVP, specify:
- What it does (behaviour, not technology)
- Who it is for (the committed segment, not everyone)
- What it does NOT do (explicit non-goals — these prevent scope creep)
- How it tests the riskiest assumption

**The 3-year product.** What does the full product look like if the MVP validates the insight and the team executes well? This is not a roadmap. It is a description of the product's destination: what problem does it solve at scale, for which customers, through what mechanism?

**Mission mapping.** For both MVP and 3-year product: how does each stage advance the company's North Star? If the MVP is a means-to-an-end (a learning vehicle) that only maps to mission at year 3, say so explicitly. Leadership needs to understand what they are funding and why.

> **Format:**
>
> **MVP:**
> - Does: [What it does — behaviour, not technology]
> - For: [The committed segment, specifically]
> - Does NOT do: [Explicit non-goals]
> - Tests: [The riskiest assumption it validates or invalidates]
> - Time to ship: [Realistic estimate]
>
> **3-year product:** [2–3 sentences. What does this look like at scale?]
>
> **Mission mapping:**
> | Stage | How it advances the North Star |
> |---|---|
> | MVP | [Specific mechanism] |
> | 3-year | [Specific mechanism] |

---

## M — Measure

*Define what success looks like. Commit to the hypothesis confirmation metric, the long-term North Star, and the guardrails you will not sacrifice.*

### What this phase delivers

**MVP hypothesis metric.** One metric. The single signal that confirms or denies whether the riskiest assumption was correct. This is not a vanity metric (downloads, sign-ups). It is a behaviour change in the committed segment that would only occur if the insight was right. State the threshold: "If [metric] reaches [X] within [timeframe], the hypothesis is confirmed."

**Long-term North Star.** The causal metric that, if it moves, proves the 3-year product is delivering its stated outcome. Must be incremental (measuring actual value added) not attributed (measuring activity that might or might not represent value). Name what makes it causal.

**Guardrails.** The metrics that must not degrade. In a two-sided market: the metric that protects the other side. At the platform level: the metric that protects the company's existing North Star from being eroded by this new initiative. Name them before the experiment runs. A guardrail without a specific threshold is not a guardrail — it is a promise to have a meeting.

> **Format:**
>
> **MVP hypothesis metric:** [Metric name] — if it reaches [threshold] within [timeframe], the hypothesis is confirmed.
>
> **Long-term North Star:** [Metric name + why it is causal, not attributed]
>
> **Guardrails:**
> | Guardrail | Threshold | What it protects |
> |---|---|---|
> | [Metric] | [Must not fall below X] | [The asset or stakeholder being protected] |

## Putting It Together: The PRISM Brief

After running all five phases, assemble a one-page brief:

```
PRISM Brief: [Initiative Name] for [Company]

P — The problem: [2 sentences. What fails, for whom.]
    Mission fit: [1 sentence. Strengthens / dilutes / orthogonal to North Star.]
    Opportunity: [Bottom-up sizing with epistemic labels.]

R — Committed segment: [Name + pain + switching motivation]
    In-house advantage: [The unfair advantage in 1 sentence]
    Build / Buy: [Decision + rationale in 2 sentences]

I — The insight: [One sentence. Non-obvious. Falsifiable.]
    Riskiest assumption: [What would prove it wrong]

S — MVP: [What it does / for whom / what it tests]
    3-year: [What it becomes at scale]

M — Hypothesis metric: [Metric + threshold + timeframe]
    North Star: [Long-term causal metric]
    Guardrails: [The 1–2 metrics that must not degrade]
```

---

## Tone and Standards

- **Commit.** PRISM forces convergence. Presenting three equally weighted options is not an answer — it is analysis paralysis dressed up as rigour. By Insights, you have chosen one segment and one insight. By Solution, you have a specific MVP. By Measure, you have a number.
- **Epistemic honesty.** Label every significant claim: [KNOWN] / [BELIEVED] / [ASSUMED]. An [ASSUMED] opportunity size presented as a forecast is not a strategy — it is wishful thinking.
- **Mission first.** Every section grounds back to the company's North Star. A product that generates revenue but dilutes the mission is a trap. Name it.
- **Two-sided market awareness.** If the company operates a two-sided market, name the guardrail that protects the subsidised side. Every new product decision is also a trust decision with the side you don't charge.

## Framework References

**Teresa Torres — *Continuous Discovery Habits***: Opportunity-first framing from R (segments and pain points) through I (the insight as the validated opportunity). Solutions trail validated insights, not the other way around.

**Marty Cagan — *Inspired***: Four Risks as the check behind the build/buy decision in R and the MVP design in S. Value risk and viability risk are the most common PRISM killers.

**Melissa Perri — *Escaping the Build Trap***: The MVP in S is a learning vehicle, not a product launch. Measure is outcome-first. The failure condition in P is Perri's antidote to the build trap.

**Hamilton Helmer — *7 Powers***: Informing the in-house advantage analysis in R. Counter-positioning, network effects, and switching costs are the most relevant powers for evaluating whether a new product is defensible.
