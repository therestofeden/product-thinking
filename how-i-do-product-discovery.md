# How I Do Product Discovery and Write a Product Master Doc

*A walkthrough of the UIE framework — Understand, Identify, Execute — the method I use before a single line of spec gets written. Starting from mission alignment, through epistemic honesty and opportunity mapping, to kill switches and a product document that states what failure looks like before it defines success.*

---

I recently led the discovery process for a new advertising product at a major European e-commerce platform. The product — let's call it Discovery Ads — proposed extending a performance advertising format from its current home in search results into the rest of the platform: the home feed, recommendation carousels, the shopping cart, filtered catalog pages. A significant initiative. A large financial opportunity on paper. And, like most initiatives that look compelling on paper, full of assumptions that could kill it quietly if left unexamined.

This is the method I used to work through it. Not the product — the method.

---

## The UIE Framework

UIE stands for **Understand → Identify → Execute**. It is the spine of how I approach every product initiative before a spec gets written.

Each phase has a specific job. They run in sequence. Skipping one doesn't save time — it creates debt that shows up later as misaligned specs, failed experiments, or products that ship but don't move the metric that actually matters.

**Understand** — Build a deep, honest picture of the system, the prior evidence, and the market before you name a single opportunity. This phase answers: *what do we actually know, and what do we only believe or assume?* Its output is an epistemic inventory and a precise product outcome statement.

**Identify** — Map the opportunity space from the outcome downward. Every opportunity is a value gap: a specific place where the product fails to deliver its stated outcome for a specific customer, with a hypothesis about why. This phase answers: *where specifically does the product fail, and for whom?* Its output is an Opportunity Solution Tree with evidence labels and risk assessments for each opportunity.

**Execute** — Prioritise interventions, design the validation approach, and define what failure looks like before the experiment launches. This phase answers: *what do we do first, how do we test it, and when do we stop?* Its output is a scored initiative table, experiment design principles, per-phase kill switches, and an Open Risks Register.

Before UIE begins, there is a prerequisite: **Step Zero**. The Mission Stack. No phase is valid without it.

---

## Step Zero: The Mission Stack

Before UIE starts — before I open a document, before I look at data — I establish why the product exists and what the company optimises for. This step looks philosophical. It is actually the most load-bearing thing I do.

Three questions, in sequence:

**Why does this product exist?** Not what it does — why it exists. What would be worse in the world if this team stopped showing up?

**What is the North Star?** Not a KPI. The single condition that, if achieved, means the mission is being fulfilled — and that is causally connected to the company's own North Star, not a local metric that looks good in team reviews but is orthogonal to what the business actually optimises for.

**What does success look like for each side of the market?** In a two-sided marketplace this question is not optional. A business that monetises attention generated by consumers serves two distinct customers — the end users whose behaviour creates the inventory, and the partners who pay to reach them. These interests are not automatically aligned. The product that maximises short-term partner revenue by saturating every surface with ads will eventually destroy the end-user experience that makes those surfaces valuable. The North Star has to hold both sides simultaneously, or it will optimise one at the expense of the other.

This is the structural constraint that every product decision on a two-sided platform is made within — and if you don't state it explicitly before discovery begins, you will make tradeoffs implicitly, inconsistently, and invisibly.

### How I apply this in practice

For Discovery Ads, the mission was something like: *help brand partners grow their business on the platform by enabling them to reach the right users with the right content at the right time*. That is the partner side. But the platform's own North Star is commerce — it exists to connect shoppers with products they want to buy and to make that experience excellent. The advertising product succeeds when it serves both: when it helps partners grow, and when it does so in a way that makes the shopping experience better or at worst neutral.

That three-way relationship — platform mission, partner success, shopper experience — is what I write down before anything else:

| Layer | Statement |
|---|---|
| Platform North Star | Connect shoppers with products they want — make that experience excellent |
| Advertising product mission | Help brand partners drive incremental growth by reaching purchase-ready shoppers |
| Product success condition | Partner incremental ROAS is positive AND shopper conversion rate is not harmed |
| What "failure" looks like | High attributed partner revenue + degraded shopper conversion = build trap entry |

**That last row is the one most product documents leave out.** A retail media product can generate impressive attributed revenue while quietly destroying the shopper experience that makes the inventory worth buying. Attribution is a lagging, partial signal. Shopper conversion is the leading, honest one. If I optimise for attributed partner revenue without a guardrail on shopper conversion, I am optimising for the map, not the territory.

Only once I have this table written do I move to the outcome sentence. The outcome sentence is then *derivable* — it follows logically from the mission stack — rather than invented to fit the initiative.

For Discovery Ads: *reach purchase-bound shoppers on surfaces where we previously had no presence, and demonstrate that this reach creates genuine incremental value for partners — without degrading the conversion quality that makes those surfaces worth monetising at all.*

Every decision that followed — which surfaces to prioritise, which metrics to treat as guardrails, where to put kill switches — derived from that stack. Not from the financial model. Not from what the team could build. From why we exist.

This step takes a few hours. It saves weeks of misaligned speccing.

## U — Understand

*Build a deep, honest picture of the system, the prior evidence, and the market before you name a single opportunity.*

The first trap in product work is treating the problem definition as settled. A VP says "we should expand our ads beyond search." An analyst produces a deck showing unmonetised surfaces. A financial model appears projecting tens of millions in incremental revenue. Everyone nods. Engineering asks when to start.

This is a map. It may or may not correspond to the territory.

### What the Understand phase delivers

Two things, in this order:

**1. The system description.** The core mechanism — the formula, rule, or process — through which this initiative's changes would translate into outcomes. Not a feature list. The mechanism. If parameter X is wrong, what happens to which customer? If signal Y is missing, what is the downstream consequence? This is what tells you where improvements actually move the needle.

**2. The epistemic inventory.** Before I write anything that looks like a spec, I spend time on one question: *what do we actually know, versus what do we believe, versus what are we assuming?* I borrow a labeling convention from the scientific literature and apply it to every significant claim:

- **[KNOWN]** — empirically validated. A randomised controlled experiment with a clean holdout. A peer-reviewed finding. A number from a database that has been QA'd. If someone challenges it, you can show them the methodology.
- **[BELIEVED]** — theory-grounded and proxy-evidenced. We have a mechanism and analogous evidence, but we haven't validated it directly at this system or at this scale.
- **[ASSUMED]** — implicit and unvalidated. Required for the logic to hold, but not yet examined. These are the claims that will hurt you most if they turn out to be wrong.

### The epistemic inventory in practice

On Discovery Ads, when I ran this exercise on the financial model, here is what I found:

The coverage gap — roughly 60% of purchase-bound sessions occur on surfaces we don't currently monetise — was **[KNOWN]**. We had the platform session data. We had traced user paths from session entry to purchase across three months of data, event by event. The number was robust.

The conservative financial projection — €25M in incremental ad revenue over three months — was **[BELIEVED]**. We applied a 50% CVR penalty to account for loose-intent surfaces converting differently than search. But the 50% was a judgment call, not a measurement. We had never run ads on the home feed or the cart. We called it conservative, but "conservative" in the absence of any baseline is just a more palatable kind of assumption.

The pilot partner quote — "35% increase in incremental revenue at stable ROAS" — was **[ASSUMED]**. A single quote from a single managed-service partner. Not independently validated, not from a randomised holdout, not reproducible as evidence.

Most product documents I encounter don't distinguish between these three. The financial model is presented as a forecast. The pilot quote is cited as validation. The assumed CVR is labeled conservative. And then when the experiment runs and the numbers don't match, the team is surprised — because they never knew what kind of claim they were making.

The labeling forces intellectual honesty before commitment. It also tells you exactly where discovery work needs to go next: toward the [ASSUMED] claims, which are the ones that will hurt you most.

### The product outcome statement

Understand ends with a single sentence. One sentence that defines what the product is supposed to accomplish — for a specific customer, in a way that is measurable, framed as an outcome and not an output. It is not invented; it is derived from the Mission Stack.

The outcome sentence format:

> *[Product] enables [customer] to [do what], such that [measurable condition], while [constraint that protects the other side of the market or the platform].*

For Discovery Ads, the instinct was to write: "Discovery Ads extends SPA to new surfaces." That is an output. It describes what gets shipped, not what changes in the world. Marty Cagan calls this the output trap. Melissa Perri gives it a name: the build trap. Optimising for what you ship rather than what you change.

The outcome I arrived at: *Discovery Ads enables brand partners to reach purchase-bound shoppers across the full shopping journey — not just in search contexts — such that partners can drive incremental conversions on surfaces where performance advertising did not previously exist, while maintaining the ROAS efficiency that makes reinvestment rational.*

Every word is load-bearing. "Purchase-bound" — not all shoppers. "Incremental conversions" — not attributed GMV. "Surfaces where performance advertising did not previously exist" — the counterfactual is explicit. "ROAS efficiency that makes reinvestment rational" — the advertiser's flywheel is in the definition.

This sentence then does something powerful: it tells you what you are *not* trying to do. You are not trying to maximise impressions. Not trying to maximise attributed revenue. The outcome definition is a scope filter.

## I — Identify

*Map the opportunity space from the outcome downward. Opportunities as value gaps, not feature requests.*

Once the outcome statement is written, I build what Teresa Torres calls an Opportunity Solution Tree. I use it because it enforces a discipline that is easy to violate: opportunities must be validated before solutions are considered, and solutions must be linked explicitly to specific validated opportunities.

The OST starts with the desired outcome at the root. From there I identify opportunities. And I am strict about what counts as an opportunity.

### What the Identify phase delivers

A structured map of where the product fails to deliver its stated outcome, with evidence and risk assessments for each gap.

**An opportunity is a value gap.** A specific place where the product fails to deliver its stated outcome for a specific customer segment, with a hypothesis about why. It is not a feature. It is not a solution. It is not a capability the team wants to build.

The framing test:
- "Add cart placement" — solution, not an opportunity
- "53% of purchase-bound events occur on a surface the product cannot reach, leaving the highest-intent moment in the customer journey completely unaddressed" — opportunity. Names the gap, the customer state, the mechanism, the downstream consequence.

This distinction matters enormously for prioritisation. When you frame opportunities as value gaps, you can compare them honestly. The home feed opportunity addresses 14 million user sessions at the entry point of the shopping journey. The cart opportunity addresses 53% of pre-purchase events but introduces checkout abandonment risk that could permanently disable the surface. These have materially different risk profiles — and you can only see that clearly if you've framed them as opportunities, not as a feature list.

### Four Risks as the gate before speccing

For each opportunity I require the same four things (from Cagan's *Inspired*):

1. **The gap**: what specifically fails, for whom, through what mechanism, with what downstream consequence.
2. **The evidence**: labeled [KNOWN], [BELIEVED], or [ASSUMED].
3. **The Four Risks**: Value / Usability / Feasibility / Viability — each rated H/M/L. Any High rating without a named mitigation path is a blocker to speccing. The opportunity does not advance.
4. **The riskiest assumption**: the single assumption that, if wrong, invalidates the solution-opportunity link entirely. Not the full assumption register — the one that kills the proposal if it fails.

For the cart placement, the riskiest assumption was not "will advertisers pay for it?" or "can we build it?" The riskiest assumption was: *adding a sponsored product carousel to the shopping cart does not meaningfully increase checkout abandonment.* That assumption, if wrong, does not reduce ad revenue — it damages core commerce. Getting that wrong at scale is irreversible in a way that most product failures are not. Naming it clearly, before building, is the only way to make sure the experiment is designed to test it.

### Two-sided market discipline

For every opportunity in a two-sided market: ask which side it primarily serves, and what the impact is on the other side. An opportunity that creates value for partners at the expense of end-user experience is not a valid opportunity — it is a failure condition entry point (see Step Zero).

## E — Execute

*Prioritise interventions, design the validation approach, and define what failure looks like before the experiment launches.*

### What the Execute phase delivers

Three things, none of which is a spec:

**1. Prioritised initiative scoring.** A table comparing solutions on evidence level, expected impact, effort, and priority. One row per solution, linked explicitly back to the opportunity it addresses. Expected impact carries epistemic labels — "TBD with science" is the correct answer when you don't know; an unknown is better labeled than fabricated.

When one phase concentrates most of the financial upside, name it explicitly. In Discovery Ads, 79% of the projected upside lived in Phase 3 (Cart). Once you see that, the prioritisation logic changes. Phase 1 is not "first because it's lower risk and a good starting point." Phase 1 is the validation sandbox that determines whether the prediction model is mature enough to operate context-free — because if it isn't, Phase 3 should not launch, and Phase 3 is where all the value is. The document should say so plainly.

**2. Kill switches.** For each phase of a phased rollout: a pre-agreed threshold, documented before the experiment launches, that triggers a specific action if crossed. Not "we'll monitor and adjust." A specific number. A specific action. A named owner.

On Discovery Ads, the kill switch structure:

**Phase 1 (Home Feed)**: If CTR or CVR on new placements is more than 50% below the search baseline, pause and diagnose. Do not advance to Phase 3 regardless of timeline pressure.

**Phase 2 (Catalog Filter Bypass)**: If any device shows a measurable signal of filter confusion — users resetting filters immediately after ad exposure, exit rates elevating — Phase 2 halts. The design must be re-validated before proceeding.

**Phase 3 (Cart)**: A pre-agreed checkout abandonment threshold — the specific number to be defined with Finance and Platform before Phase 3 launches — triggers immediate disablement. As of the discovery document I wrote, that threshold was an open blocker. It did not yet exist. The document said so in unambiguous terms. Not because it was embarrassing, but because unnamed blockers are the things that slip through and become production incidents.

**3. The Open Risks Register.** Every significant risk goes into a table with severity, owner, status, and next action. This is a *prospective* inventory — asking "what are the most likely ways this fails?" before you build anything. The highest-severity risks are called out explicitly, not buried in footnotes. High severity without a mitigation path is a blocker, not a risk.

### The financial model as a belief system

Product financial models are almost always presented as forecasts. They look precise. They are belief systems. The discipline is to treat them as such.

The honest framing: here are the conditions under which this initiative is worth pursuing, here is what we need to be true about conversion on new surfaces, and here is what the experiment needs to tell us before we make any financial commitment at scale. The model's job is not to predict the outcome — it is to make the assumptions explicit enough that you know which ones the experiment needs to test.

## The Product Master Doc

The output of UIE is not a deck. It is not a PRD. It is a Product Master Doc — the team's continuously maintained picture of what we know, what we're betting on, and what failure looks like.

### What it IS

- **It states the outcome before naming any solution.** Every reader — engineer, scientist, exec — starts from the same sentence about what the product is supposed to accomplish.
- **It makes the epistemic inventory visible.** [KNOWN], [BELIEVED], [ASSUMED] on every major claim. This is the alignment layer between the map and the territory.
- **It maps the opportunity space from the outcome down.** Every opportunity is traceable to the outcome statement. Every solution is traceable to a specific validated opportunity. If you can't draw that line, the discovery is incomplete.
- **It defines failure before success.** Kill switches for every phase. Metric hierarchy with guardrails, not just primary metrics.
- **It documents what the team doesn't know.** Open Risks Register with severity, owner, status, and next action.
- **It is a living document** until the pilot reads out. Every phase gate updates it. Every open risk that closes or escalates updates it.

### What it is NOT

- A PRD — no acceptance criteria, no implementation detail, no sprint planning
- A solution proposal — solutions appear as hypotheses only, behind validated opportunities
- A strategy deck — no market size slides, no competitive landscape
- An experiment plan — experiment design principles only; the full experiment design is a separate document

### Document structure

```
Step Zero: Mission Stack
  — Platform North Star / product mission / success condition / failure condition

Context: Product Outcome
  — Outcome sentence (derived from Mission Stack, not invented)
  — Metric hierarchy: primary (causal) / secondary / guardrail / leading indicator

U — Understand
  — The System: the mechanism through which changes produce outcomes
  — What We Know: epistemic inventory, organised by body of evidence
    Each claim labeled [KNOWN] / [BELIEVED] / [ASSUMED]

I — Identify
  — Opportunity Solution Tree
    Desired outcome box (verbatim restatement)
    Each opportunity: gap / evidence / Four Risks / riskiest assumption / risks

E — Execute
  — Initiative Scoring: solutions ranked on evidence / impact / effort / priority
  — Kill Switches: per-phase threshold table, owner, action
  — Experiment Design Principles: methodology / randomisation / success hierarchy / decision rule
  — Open Risks Register: severity / owner / status / next action

Appendix
  — Financial model with epistemic labels on every assumption
  — Source materials
```

## Why This Works

The real function of UIE is to protect the team from two failure modes more common than building the wrong feature:

**False precision** — presenting [ASSUMED] claims as [KNOWN] ones, financial projections as forecasts, convenient numbers as evidence. False precision produces confident roadmaps for products that fail, in a way that makes the failure hard to diagnose after the fact because no one ever wrote down what was actually assumed.

**Premature commitment** — starting to spec and build before the riskiest assumptions have been tested. The build trap, as Perri describes it, is not entered when you build the wrong thing. It is entered at the moment you commit to building before you've validated the opportunity. The Product Master Doc is the mechanism for delaying that commitment until it's earned.

## The Frameworks, Named

UIE synthesises three books into a single method. None of them works in isolation.

**Marty Cagan — *Inspired*.** The Four Risks framework (Value, Usability, Feasibility, Viability) as a gate before speccing. The insistence that discovery and delivery run in parallel, not sequentially. The missionary vs. mercenary distinction — a product team with a clear mission makes categorically better decisions than a team executing a stakeholder wish list.

**Teresa Torres — *Continuous Discovery Habits*.** The Opportunity Solution Tree as the default structure for mapping a problem space. The discipline of opportunity-first framing — solutions are hypotheses, not commitments, until the opportunity is validated. The habit of continuous research as the source of opportunities, not stakeholder handoffs.

**Melissa Perri — *Escaping the Build Trap*.** The outcome-over-output framing. The Product Kata. The recognition that the build trap is entered not at the moment of building but at the moment of premature commitment — and that the antidote is outcome clarity before solution commitment.

**Where UIE differs from any one of them**: Torres' OST doesn't have a financial model discipline or kill switches. Cagan's Four Risks don't include epistemic labeling of claims. Perri's outcome framing doesn't have a structured opportunity tree with evidence requirements. UIE combines all three, adds the Mission Stack as a prerequisite, and adds the Open Risks Register as a prospective failure inventory. The method is mine — it borrows from all of them and is identical to none.
